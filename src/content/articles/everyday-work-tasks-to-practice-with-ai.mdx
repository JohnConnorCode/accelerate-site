If you have used AI to write an email, the next step can be another small piece of work you already understand. Familiarity matters: you can tell whether the result is useful, what is missing, and what needs correcting.

Here are three exercises you can try with an AI assistant. All the details are fictional. Start with the one that resembles your work, and keep the example small enough to check yourself.

## 1. Prepare for a meeting

**The task:** Turn a few known facts into an agenda and questions. The aim is to arrive prepared, with a clear view of what the meeting needs to decide.

Try this request:

```text
Help me prepare for a 20-minute project check-in.

Known facts:
- The first website draft is ready for review.
- The client still needs to choose their photos.
- The launch date has not been agreed.

Create a short agenda with a suggested time for each item.
Then write three questions that help us decide the next steps.
Keep unanswered questions open. Do not invent decisions.
```

**Check the result:** The suggested times should fit the meeting. The questions should address the missing photos, feedback on the draft, and what is needed before agreeing on a launch date. Remove anything that promises work you have not agreed to do.

**Try a follow-up:** “Help me phrase the question about missing photos politely and directly.”

## 2. Organize rough notes

**The task:** Make a short set of notes easier to review. You are looking for structure: actions, owners, dates, and open questions.

```text
Organize these fictional notes into a table with columns for
action, owner, due date, and open question.

Notes:
Maya will send the revised menu on Thursday.
We still need someone to confirm the room setup.
Jordan asked whether we can print 30 copies of the handout.
No one has approved the printing cost yet.

Use only what is stated. Write "not agreed" when an owner
or date is missing. Keep questions separate from commitments.
```

**Check the result:** Maya has an action and a day. The room setup has no owner or deadline yet. Jordan’s question is not approval to spend money. Those distinctions should survive the formatting.

**Try a follow-up:** “List only the decisions I need to confirm before assigning the remaining work.”

For a real meeting, compare the table with the original notes before sharing it. Clear formatting can make an invented commitment easy to overlook.

<Callout type="tip" title="Use AI to make the gaps visible">
  An empty owner or an unanswered question can be useful. Ask the assistant to identify missing
  information instead of filling it in.
</Callout>

## 3. Outline a weekly update

**The task:** Make an update easier for a colleague to scan. Supply the facts and ask for a consistent structure.

```text
Help me turn these fictional facts into a short weekly update.

Completed: reviewed the website draft with the team.
In progress: collecting photos for the product pages.
Blocked: we need a decision on the launch date.
Next step: ask the client to confirm their photo selections.

Use four headings: Completed, In progress, Blocked, Next steps.
Keep the language plain. Do not add progress percentages,
deadlines, or claims that are not in the notes.
```

**Check the result:** The update should distinguish completed work from work still underway. It should not claim the project is “almost finished” or suggest a launch date. Decide whether the next step needs an owner before you send the update.

**Try a follow-up:** “Make this shorter without losing the blocker or the next step.”

## Choose one exercise and finish it

You do not have to learn every use for AI at once. Work through one request, one follow-up, and one review. Ask yourself what improved and what still needed your judgment.

Use fictional details while practicing, and follow your organization’s rules before entering workplace information.
